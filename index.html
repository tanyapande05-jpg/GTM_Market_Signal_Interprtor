The Raw Material

Building a drone is a systems problem. Sourcing components, integrating subsystems, getting to a first stable flight. Every layer depends on the one beneath it. It taught me to think in dependencies before outcomes.

We built a DIY drone kit, then our own flight controller and remote controller. Over 18 months: three flight controller iterations and INR 4 lakhs of indigenous R&D. Real engineering, real execution.

We were aerospace engineers building the core of a drone ecosystem, solving a problem that was real but not urgent. That gap between real and urgent is the first pattern I now look for in any venture. Back then I was living inside it; today I name it on sight. Pattern I now name on sight

The Pattern Hiding In Plain Sight

Our Build Your Own Drone workshops kept working. Parents referred other parents. Teachers reached out. Revenue came in consistently. The pull was quiet, repeating, and pointed in one clear direction.

But DJI was our north star, so we read ourselves as a product company and kept pushing a Rs. 5,000 kit into a race to the bottom against imported drones. The signal was steady; we were facing the other way. This exact mismatch, consistent pull versus the story you want to be true, is what the Signal Interpreter below is built to catch in seconds. Now a diagnostic

The Question That Became A System

My father asked one question: "What is actually bringing in revenue?"

It cut through 18 months of narrative. The workshops had been the answer from day one. The kit was the instrument; the experience was the product. We were a B2B2C hardware plus edtech company, not a D2C hardware company. But the real move wasn't learning that once. It was turning it into a reusable test. That single question is now the revenue truth test inside this tool. Lived insight is a story; a repeatable diagnostic is a system. Real signal, systematized

What The Dataset Is, Honestly

One venture, three business models, three GTM motions: D2C, B2B, then B2B2C. Plus a hardware company and an edtech company running inside the same P&L. Not three companies. One company that forced me to learn three ways of reading a market, and to notice when the outcomes of one model were being used to validate the other.

The founder years gave me the data. The venture-builder work is what I do with it: extract the pattern, encode it so someone else can run it without living through 18 months first, and then test it where it counts, on ventures that aren't mine.

Where I run this read on other companies: teardowns and 0-to-1 memos on consumer physical products, early-stage medical hardware, and the economics of the drone industry. Linked here as they publish.

Step 4 of 5
6 questions. Be honest.
No one is watching. These reveal how you are unconsciously filtering what you see.
1. Are your customers coming for you personally, or for the product or service?
2. Did you avoid asking a follow-up question in a customer conversation because you were afraid of what the answer might be?
3. Is your most consistent, quiet signal, the one that keeps showing up without you chasing it, getting less attention than a louder but one-off signal?
4. If your product disappeared tomorrow, would this customer change their workflow, or just go back to what they did before?
5. Why are your first 10 customers buying, and would the next 100 buy for the same reason?
6. If a competitor copies your product exactly and sells it for 30% less, what happens to your customers?
